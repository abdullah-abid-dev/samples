#####Clinicians and therapists

Motor impairment in autism is still easy to miss, including by trained professionals.

Dr. Antigone Oreopoulos, a physiotherapist and researcher in Edmonton, puts it plainly on The Lighter Side of the Spectrum: seeing someone walk or climb stairs is not a motor assessment. The foundational gaps often show up in crawling, balance, sit-to-stand, ball skills, and the ability to start, grade, and reverse a movement.

She cites SPARK data from Anjana Bhat: motor impairment in at least 90% of autistic people, and worse impairment where functional speech is absent. Standard DCD tools often floor out with nonspeaking children, so a score below the first percentile does not give the team a useful baseline.

Her caution for IQ and “nonverbal” tests is sharper. If the method is pointing or eye gaze, and there is no reliable communication, the test may be measuring motor control. The cerebral palsy literature already treats that as invalid. Educational placement and funding should not rest on it.

The episode is a useful listen for PTs, OTs, SLPs, and psychologists who work with nonspeaking autistic people. Two practices she uses immediately: give more breaks than seem necessary, and perform the motor skill yourself before you teach the breakdown.

#####Parents and caregivers

A child can understand the request and still be unable to do it.

Physiotherapist Dr. Antigone Oreopoulos talks with David Kaufer about how that gap gets recorded as noncompliance, behavior, or low comprehension. Her son Stellan spells on a letterboard. The spellers she quotes describe a brain that knows the plan and a body that daydreams, loops, or knocks over the cup.

For parents, the episode is both technical and personal. Antigone talks about six months of ABA because a social worker directed her there, about disciplining a “won’t” that was a “can’t,” and about the line she now uses: know better, do better.

She also offers a workable starting point. You do not have to wait for a perfect test. Look at early motor milestones. Ask for the motor impairment to be documented even if a DCD label is hard to get. Bring in a developmental optometrist when you can. And if speech and intent do not match, verify with the reliable method, not with the body’s first action.

#####Researchers, educators, and policy

Diagnostic language still pushes autism through a social and behavioral frame. The motor evidence is not new, and practice has not caught up.

On The Lighter Side of the Spectrum, Dr. Antigone Oreopoulos argues for motor impairment as a core descriptor, not only a dual diagnosis of autism plus DCD. She walks through why that matters: intervention design, invalid cognitive testing, delayed publicly funded assessment in Canada, and adults who age with untreated weakness, pain, and no way to report it.

She also names the training gap. Coursework that mentions DCD is not the same as learning from communicative nonspeakers and from clinicians who have done the work for years. She wants a mentor network: parent to parent, practitioner to practitioner, and practitioner to self-advocate.

Canada’s National Autism Network comes up as one attempt to close that knowledge-to-action gap. The episode is a clear account of what gets lost while that gap stays open.
