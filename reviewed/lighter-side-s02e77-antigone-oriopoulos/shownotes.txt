The Lighter Side of the Spectrum
Rethinking Autism Through a Sensory-Motor Lens, with Dr. Antigone Oreopoulos

David talks with Dr. Antigone Oreopoulos, a physiotherapist, researcher, disability advocate, and mom in Edmonton, Alberta. Her son Stellan is nonspeaking, uses a letterboard, and was about to turn 11. They talk about the gap between intention and movement, why that gap gets misread as refusal or lack of understanding, and what changes when communication is treated as a motor task.

In this episode

Why the body is part of the conversation for nonspeaking and unreliably speaking autistic people
How Antigone left physiotherapy, completed a PhD in public health, and later returned to practice after recognizing her son’s sensory-motor impairment
What the DSM leaves out, and why motor impairment is being argued for as a core descriptor
Peyton Thorpe and Marcus Hulette, in their own words, on a body that will not follow the brain
How ABA compliance scoring can punish a person for a movement they cannot reliably make
SPARK findings, via Anjana Bhat: motor impairment in at least 90% of autistic people, and worse motor scores where functional speech is absent
Why standard DCD tests often fail nonspeaking children, and what a motor check can include instead: milestones, plus Elizabeth Torres’s sitting, pointing, and walking measures
What gets missed when walking and stair-climbing are treated as proof that physiotherapy is unnecessary
Why pointing to a letterboard is a complex motor sequence, not “just pointing”
Why cognitive and IQ tests that rely on pointing or eye gaze are invalid without a reliable means of communication, and why the least dangerous assumption is presumed competence
Apraxia, dyspraxia, and DCD, and why the split terminology gets in the way
Swimming, gymnastics, trampoline, and modified CrossFit as ways to build motor control and raise heart rate
Canada’s National Autism Network, and the shortage of practitioners trained by nonspeakers and experienced mentors
Tip of the week

Give more breaks than you think are needed. If the person cannot say when they need one, set a timer.
Before you teach a motor skill, do it yourself and notice the steps your own body is taking.
Fact or fiction

Motor challenges are usually obvious to trained professionals. Antigone’s answer: often they are not. She separates “Stellan the person” from “Stellan the body,” and coaches the body once the instruction has not been carried out.

Parent confession

Antigone describes disciplining Stellan for dumping Lego when what she was seeing was “can’t,” not “won’t,” and choosing not to offer experiences she assumed would be too hard. David connects that to his own moments with Stone.

About the guest

Dr. Antigone Oreopoulos is a physiotherapist, researcher, inclusion specialist, consultant, and coach. She supports people with sensory-motor impairment, including nonspeaking autistic people, and advocates for earlier physiotherapy, motor-based communication, and practitioner training that includes nonspeaking mentors. She previously competed in trampoline for Alberta and teaches swimming and gymnastics. Her website was not ready at recording. She offered an email for questions and consults.
