When understanding is present and the body does not follow

Communication is usually treated as a language question. Can the person understand? Can they speak? On a recent episode of The Lighter Side of the Spectrum, host David Kaufer and physiotherapist Dr. Antigone Oreopoulos start somewhere else: a person may understand the request exactly, and still be unable to make the body carry it out. That gap is often recorded as refusal, noncompliance, or a lack of comprehension. For many nonspeaking and unreliably speaking autistic people, it is a sensory-motor and motor-planning problem. The body is part of the conversation.

Dr. Oreopoulos is a physiotherapist, researcher, disability advocate, and inclusion specialist based in Edmonton, Alberta. She also brings a less usual mix of training: competitive trampoline, gymnastics coaching, and swim instruction. Her son, Stellan, is nonspeaking and spells on a letterboard. He was diagnosed at 3. The letterboard came, as it does for many families, by accident, when he was 4 and three-quarters. She had already been told he had little to no receptive language and was severely cognitively delayed. Spelling required her to set that account aside.

Her route back into clinical work ran through that experience. She trained first as a physiotherapist, moved into research and a PhD in public health, and let her license go. After she came to see her son’s primary challenge as sensory-motor impairment, she saw a population that physiotherapy was largely missing. She regained her license and now works with families directly, while still homeschooling and caregiving.

A definition that still misses movement

A large part of the episode is about why this remains so hard to see. North American diagnosis still runs through the DSM, and the criteria emphasize social communication and social interaction. The language around behavior implies intent. Dr. Oreopoulos contrasts that with the way other neurological conditions are read. When someone with Parkinson’s struggles to move, the struggle is not treated as a behavior to reduce. Autism is still routinely read through a social lens.

She is direct about the word “behavior.” Outside autism it can be a neutral description. Inside autism it arrives already loaded. She also points to evidence that sensory-motor impairment helps explain the social and communication differences clinicians observe, and to researchers who want motor impairment added to the DSM as a core descriptor. It is not there yet, and she says it is still absent from most people’s working picture of autism, even though the motor research is not new. The larger failure, in her account, is translation from knowledge into practice.

The clearest description in the episode does not come from a clinician. Dr. Oreopoulos reads two young autistic spellers from Edmonton, Peyton Thorpe and Marcus Hulette, both 21 when they wrote the passages about a year before the recording. Peyton describes a body with a mind of its own, a constant fight to locate himself in space, interrupt motor loops, and make a movement smooth. His image is a race in which the brain watches from the stands while the body spins and runs backward: “My brain understands, but it’s like my body is daydreaming.” Marcus takes the image up. His body is “like a puppy that just watched a rabbit run by.” It knows the task. Reaching for a glass, the hand is not there. It is flapping beside the head. When it finally moves, it knocks the cup over.

David recognizes the same account from his son, Stone. The details differ from person to person. The structure does not. The brain is asking for one action, and the body is doing another, or doing nothing the person can use.

Compliance is the wrong measure

Both parent and clinician, Dr. Oreopoulos is explicit about what follows when that structure is ignored. Families are routinely steered toward a familiar triad for level 3 autism: speech therapy aimed at oral speech, behavioral therapy, and occupational therapy. She did about six months of ABA because a social worker told her to. David’s family stayed longer. Neither found that it produced communication.

The objection is specific. A program that tries to control or change behavior assumes the person has volitional control of the motor system. Without that foundation, the program does not build the missing skill. It records a failed movement as noncompliance, then attaches reward and punishment to the score. Stone later spelled that he dreaded those sessions because he knew a missed physical demand could cost him a cartoon he loved. Dr. Oreopoulos’s conclusion is blunt: without autonomous motor control, a top-down behavioral plan does little except cause trauma. Time spent there is time taken from childhood, and from therapies with a better chance of working.

She is equally plain that this is not only a parent’s complaint. As a provider who holds government contracts, she sees substantial public money continue to flow toward services that the research does not support for functional communication in nonspeaking autism.

What the motor research changes

Motor impairment in autism is still generally handled as a dual diagnosis: autism plus developmental coordination disorder. Dr. Oreopoulos walks through the SPARK findings associated with researcher Anjana Bhat. About 14,000 participants completed a DCD screening questionnaire. The figure she cites is that at least 90 percent of autistic people have motor impairment, including people with more significant support needs, and that motor impairment is worse where functional speech is absent.

The tests used to document that impairment were built before anyone understood how common and how severe it is in this group. Dr. Oreopoulos has administered standardized motor assessments to children like her son and finds the same result again and again: scores fall below the first percentile. The floor of the test is too high to give a useful baseline, and a formal DCD diagnosis can be nearly impossible to obtain in nonspeaking autism. She does not think every family needs that label. She does think the motor impairment has to be written down, so the team stops planning as if it is absent.

There is still no agreed method for measuring motor impairment in level 3 autism, and few physiotherapists or occupational therapists have enough experience with nonspeaking autistic people to assess it well. In Canada, DCD in autism entered PT and OT curricula roughly eight or nine years ago, mostly at a survey level. Her own workaround is practical. She uses developmental motor milestones anyone can look up — rolling, crawling, jumping, ball skills, single-leg balance, squat, sit-to-stand — and she has begun adding three short measures from Elizabeth Torres: two minutes of sitting still, two minutes of pointing to a target, and two minutes of walking.

The stakes rise with age. Dr. Oreopoulos describes young adults with postural change from stimming and prolonged poor sitting, children who cannot move from sit to stand without their hands, and weakness through the core and upper body from too little overhead activity. Autistic people already face higher rates of co-occurring conditions, faster aging associated with inflammation, and a greater risk of Parkinsonian symptoms. Many still cannot report pain. Correcting a lost range of motion or an overuse injury later requires the person to isolate a muscle or settle into a stretch, two things that significant motor impairment makes extremely difficult. Prevention is the realistic clinical goal. Movement also supports regulation, participation, and confidence, and it feeds the motor control that spelling and typing demand.

Pointing is not a simple test of thought

Families and skeptics often treat a letterboard as a shortcut. Put it on a stand, ask the person to point, and treat the result as proof for or against independent communication. Dr. Oreopoulos rejects that picture. Speech is one output pathway. For many of these children it is difficult to reach because of the way movement developed. Pointing can be more available, because gross-motor reach comes earlier in that hierarchy, but it is still a skilled movement. It requires postural control from the trunk and pelvis, an initiation the person can feel, head control, shoulder stability, appropriate muscle tone, force grading, depth perception, and the ability to reverse direction. Reversal, she says, is widely hard in this population. The person then has to repeat the sequence letter by letter. Calling that “just pointing” understates the work.

Vision belongs in the same assessment. She notes that neurodevelopmental conditions commonly include visual-motor and oculomotor difficulty, and that four of the twelve cranial nerves serve the eyes. Her son wears tinted glasses at the letterboard because of light sensitivity, and prism glasses during gross-motor work. David describes Stone seeing the Olympic range from their beach in Edmonds, Washington, for the first time after the prescription was right. Neither a child nor a new speller can reliably announce double vision. A developmental optometrist should be part of the team when the family can find one.

The same motor facts undermine a great deal of cognitive testing. Many so-called nonverbal IQ measures still ask the person to point or to use eye gaze. “Nonverbal,” as Dr. Oreopoulos reminds listeners, means without words. These children are not without words. If they have no reliable method of communication, a pointing test cannot separate language and understanding from motor performance in that moment. She treats the result as unusable, and she notes that this limitation is already recognized for people with cerebral palsy. The tests are used anyway, for school placement and for funding. That is not a technical quibble. It sets a trajectory for a person and a family.

Her standard is the least dangerous assumption. Where speech is absent, nothing in the physiology justifies treating that absence as an absence of intelligence. Instruction should continue, academically and physically, until there is real evidence otherwise. Nonspeaking people, including people with Rett syndrome and Down syndrome as well as autism, are still regularly asked to prove capacity before they are allowed to learn. She calls that tragic, and dehumanizing.

A different clinical stance

The alternative she describes is not a proprietary program so much as a change in where the work starts. Presume competence. Document the motor impairment. Teach the missing movement instead of scoring the failed one. Separate the person from the body’s unreliable output. With her own son she gives the instruction to Stellan, and if the body does not carry it out, she shifts and coaches the body: direction, a spoken prompt, a visual prompt that stays in place after the words have disappeared, or a tactile cue. Peyton has said that this kind of coaching helps build a shorter path from intention to action.

That split also explains speech that sounds convincing and is still not a reliable guide. Stone’s intentional speech has improved and still is not perfect. David’s practice, when the words and the history do not match, is to verify on the letterboard. Dr. Oreopoulos does the same. A parent colleague of hers calls the mismatch “opposite land”: the person spells one choice, and the body does the other. Enough observation can sometimes distinguish intentional movement from unintentional movement. Often it cannot. The spelled words are the signal to trust.

Parents, in her clinical model, are not junior assistants waiting on a professional. She works with the parent and the child, and she is explicit that families do not need her degrees to become skilled support people. Making a family dependent on her service, given that the need is long-term, strikes her as unethical. The harder task is undoing years of low expectations delivered by other providers. Mindset shifts slowly, and it has to be approached carefully. The cost of leaving it in place is lost learning and a lower quality of life. The record the other way is already visible: with high expectations and effective support, her son has exceeded what she thought he could learn physically. Stone, once apraxia was understood, jumped from the diving board again and again after years in which fear had been the assumed explanation.

Terminology still gets in the way. Apraxia, dyspraxia, and DCD are used as if they were interchangeable. Dyspraxia is the term more common in the UK. Apraxia, more narrowly, refers to motor planning, which is a large part of the difficulty and not the whole of it. Dr. Oreopoulos would rather see motor impairment recognized directly than continue with a dual-diagnosis patchwork that families and clinicians cannot track.

She is also frank about professional education. Feeling well trained because of what was taught in school is not the same as learning from communicative nonspeakers, or from clinicians who have spent years beside them. She describes being corrected by the people she supports, and she argues that a book or a classroom cannot replace that. The network she wants is parent to parent, practitioner to practitioner, practitioner to self-advocate, and self-advocate to self-advocate. In Canada she points to the National Autism Network as one attempt to narrow the knowledge gap and inform a national autism strategy. David is less optimistic about an equivalent effort in the United States.

What to do with this on Monday

The episode closes with practices small enough to use immediately.

Give more breaks than you think the person needs. Effort is not always visible, and Dr. Oreopoulos has pushed someone into dysregulation by skipping them. If the person cannot request a break, she sets a timer, sometimes for five or ten minutes of work and then a minute or two of rest.

Before teaching a new motor skill, do it yourself. She learned this while teaching a client to pump a swing. The explanation she had built was missing a piece she only found by performing the movement and asking what her own muscles were doing.

Look past walking. A person who climbs stairs can still be missing milestones that matter for coordination, for later joint health, and for communication. Fitness counts too. Motor drills are not the same thing as raising the heart rate. She has adapted beginner and children’s CrossFit-style circuits for that reason, including for her son.

And treat spelled or typed language as the account to believe when speech and movement disagree. Verification is not a lack of trust in the person. It is respect for a body that cannot yet be trusted to match the person’s intent.

The episode is worth the full hour for clinicians who assess or treat nonspeaking autistic people, and for families who have been told that a failed movement was a refusal to try.
