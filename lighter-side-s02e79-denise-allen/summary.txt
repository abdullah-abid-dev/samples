The podcast host, David Kaufer, introduces the show and the guest, Denise Allen, host of the Healing Our Sight podcast. They discuss the importance of looking beyond assumptions about autism, specifically challenging the idea that vision is only about seeing clearly. Denise shares her personal experience with strabismus, a condition where eyes don't focus together, and how it affected her coordination and self-esteem growing up. She explains that traditional eye exams often focus on 20/20 vision and may miss underlying binocular vision issues that impact learning and behavior.Denise details her journey with strabismus, noting that while her brother's condition was obvious at birth and treated with surgery, her own was a compensatory type that went undetected until she was nearly three. She recounts a childhood memory of being unable to see through binoculars or identify musical notes, which her doctor dismissed. Denise explains that traditional solutions like surgery or glasses often address alignment but don't teach the eyes how to work together, leading to a lack of depth perception.Denise discusses the lack of awareness and resources for vision therapy, noting that it wasn't something she knew about until she was in her 40s. She shares her daughter's story, who also had strabismus but was able to gain 3D vision through vision therapy after the host identified symptoms like double vision and covering one eye while reading. Denise emphasizes that vision therapy is a brain-training process that can be effective for children, though it requires time and effort.Denise describes her experience with strabismus surgery, which she underwent after 80 sessions of vision therapy. She chose a natural recovery approach using Arnica instead of pain medication and was able to return to therapy quickly, gaining significant depth perception. She clarifies that vision therapy does not fix acuity (20/20 vision) and that she still requires glasses. Denise notes that the success of surgery is often measured only by eye alignment, not by the ability to see in 3D.The conversation shifts to the prevalence of vision problems in the autistic population, with Denise citing statistics that 20-25% of children enter school with significant vision problems. She highlights the frustration for non-speaking individuals who may have vision issues that affect their ability to communicate or learn, as they often lack the means to express their struggles. Denise explains that vision problems can mimic ADHD, dyslexia, and sensory processing issues, leading to misdiagnosis.Denise and the host discuss the systemic lack of emphasis on vision screening in schools, attributing it to financial constraints and a lack of training among school staff. They draw parallels between the resistance to vision therapy and the resistance to alternative communication methods like letterboards for non-speaking individuals, noting that both are often dismissed due to a lack of scientific proof. Denise emphasizes the need for a holistic approach to healthcare, where different specialists collaborate rather than working in isolation.The host asks Denise for a checklist of signs parents should look for to identify potential vision issues in their children. She provides a list of red flags, including losing place while reading, skipping lines, avoiding reading, getting frustrated quickly, and being clumsy. Denise stresses the importance of seeing a developmental optometrist rather than a regular optometrist, as the latter may not screen for the specific binocular vision skills that impact learning.The episode concludes with a segment called "Tip of the Week," where Denise advises parents that a child can have clear vision but still struggle with using both eyes together. She also covers "Fact or Fiction," debunking the myth that 20/20 vision means the eyes are functioning correctly. The "Confession of the Week" features Denise sharing that she gave up on vision therapy for her son, who had convergence insufficiency, because he was resistant at age 15. The episode ends with "Light on the Spectrum," where Denise shares her personal story of gaining 3D vision at age 54 as proof of neuroplasticity, offering hope to others.
