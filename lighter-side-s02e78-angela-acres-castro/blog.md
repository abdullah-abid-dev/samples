# Trusting Your Gut: Angela Acres-Castro on Autism Advocacy and the Lighter Side of the Spectrum

In this episode of *The Lighter Side of the Spectrum*, host David Kaufer sits down with Angela Acres-Castro to explore the journey of a mother who trusted her instincts over medical skepticism. Listen to Angela's powerful story about navigating the confusion of a delayed diagnosis, challenging misconceptions about autism, and building a community in Eastern Washington that supports families living on the spectrum. This post summarizes the key insights from their conversation, offering practical advice for parents and advocates alike.

## The Journey from "Five-Star Baby" to Diagnosis
Angela Acres-Castro describes her son, Miles, as a "five-star baby" who was happy, easygoing, and always smiling. However, as he grew older, Angela began noticing behaviors that didn't fit the typical developmental trajectory. When she asked questions about these changes, she was often told not to worry, given the common advice that "boys mature more slowly." Despite this reassurance, Angela's instinct told her there was more to the story.

The path to understanding was fraught with confusion. In 2009, when Miles was diagnosed at age five, the public discourse around autism was heavily influenced by misinformation, such as the claims made by Jenny McCarthy regarding vaccines. Angela recalls reading McCarthy's book and feeling a deep connection to the narrative, even though she knew the medical consensus was different. It wasn't until she questioned her doctor about vaccine safety during her own pregnancy that she realized she needed to look for answers rather than accept pamphlets explaining why her child was different.

*   **The Initial Signs:** Miles was affectionate, sat on Angela's lap, and resisted being taken away by caregivers.
*   **The Misconceptions:** Doctors and others assumed his attachment to his mother was abnormal, stating that autistic children are not typically so bonded.
*   **The Turning Point:** Angela's refusal to accept "boys are wild" as an excuse for his developmental delays eventually led her to seek a developmental pediatrician.

## Challenging Stereotypes About Autism
One of the most impactful parts of Angela's story is how she challenged the rigid stereotypes surrounding autism. Society often assumes that autistic individuals are antisocial, lack emotion, or have severe sensory sensitivities that prevent them from enjoying certain activities. Angela's son, however, was a "social butterfly" who loved water, had a favorite babysitter's son he called "Mr. Bleekman," and maintained deep, loving relationships with his family.

Angela highlights specific behaviors that contradicted the medical narrative of the time:
*   **Attachment:** Miles ran to Angela when she picked him up from daycare and did not want to be separated from her.
*   **Social Skills:** He remembered names and faces, such as calling a teacher "Mr. Bleekman" after seeing a resemblance to a character from *Clifford the Big Red Dog*.
*   **Sensory Preferences:** While many autistic individuals dislike deep pressure, Miles initially hated a weighted vest because it felt "stuck." Instead, he found deep pressure comforting by wrapping his legs tightly for 15 minutes before spreading them out to achieve the right sensation.
*   **Dietary Variety:** Unlike the stereotype of the child who only eats chicken nuggets or rice, Miles enjoyed a variety of foods, including tomatoes, watermelon, spaghetti, salad, and cheeseburgers.

Angela notes that while Miles did engage in stimming (spinning faster than anyone she knew), he also displayed typical social behaviors that led others to believe he simply had a speech delay. Her experience underscores the importance of observing individual children rather than forcing them into a box defined by outdated assumptions.

## The Power of Asking Better Questions
A central theme of the episode is the necessity of asking better questions when the answers are elusive. Angela recounts a time when she felt dismissed by a doctor who implied her concerns were in her head. Instead of accepting this, she insisted on seeing a specialist. She stated, "I need to know what's going on," demonstrating the courage required to advocate for her child.

This approach extended beyond the medical realm. After Miles graduated from high school, Angela realized that the biggest challenges for families with developmental disabilities often begin after school ends. She didn't accept this as the natural order of things. Instead, she started asking lots of questions about how other states supported adults with disabilities. She met with lawmakers and researched systems that few people truly understand until they are living them.

*   **Researching Systems:** Angela investigated support structures in other states to find better options for her son.
*   **Community Building:** Her inquiries led to the creation of a community helping people and families across Washington State navigate the complex systems of care.
*   **Advocacy:** She continues to work with lawmakers to ensure that families receive the support they need, proving that asking questions can lead to better answers and systemic change.
